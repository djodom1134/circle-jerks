How the Runway Ledger Works A plain-language guide to the math — showing its work

The airport has no control tower, so no one keeps a log of who used the runway. Instead of estimating, this ledger counts — one aircraft, one runway pass at a time — from the same public ADS-B signals any flight-tracking site receives.

Prepared for the City of Longmont and the public. Every figure here is produced by the same code that runs the public ledger; this page explains that arithmetic and shows its work.

The problem we set out to solve

An untowered field that only had estimates

Vance Brand is an untowered airport. There is no control tower keeping a log of who used the runway or how often. When a field like this reports its traffic, the number is almost always an FAA Form 5010 estimate — a planning figure, not a count of real events. The commonly cited figure for KLMO is on the order of 120,000 operations a year, and that is exactly what it says it is: an estimate.

This ledger is different. Every airplane with an ADS-B transmitter broadcasts its position, altitude, and speed several times a second. We watch those broadcasts near the airport and record each discrete event we can positively identify. From that follows the single most important rule on the whole page:

Every number on this ledger is a floor, never an estimate. Aircraft without ADS-B are invisible to us, and coverage has gaps — so the true activity is always higher than what we report, never lower. If we cannot prove it, we do not publish it.

What we count

The “runway use”

The billable unit is a runway use — one arrival at the runway. It is the most important definition on the page, and it comes in exactly three kinds.

Landing

Runway use

The aircraft descended from at least 500 ft above the field, came within 1.5 nm of the runway at 200 ft or lower, and did not climb back out within 300 seconds.

Touch-and-go

Runway use

The aircraft flew a closed pattern loop that climbed at least 300 ft above the field and passed within 0.25 nm of the runway. This is a geometric test — so we count it as a use of the runway, not a verified touchdown.

Low approach

Runway use

The aircraft approached low and slow (200 ft or lower, 90 knots or less), spent no more than 60 seconds near the ground, and climbed back out.

Why not just count “operations”? Because an FAA operation is one takeoff or one landing — so a single touch-and-go is two operations. Multiplying a fee by an operations count would double-charge every touch-and-go on the field. Counting runway uses — arrivals only, never takeoffs — is what keeps the money math honest.

We also never double-count one lap. Because the detector scans in overlapping time windows, a single physical pattern lap can occasionally get written down twice. The tell is that the two records overlap in time — you cannot fly two seven-minute circuits that start a minute apart — so when laps overlap, we keep one and discard the rest. When in doubt we drop the lap, because an inflated count is the one mistake the ledger cannot survive.

The landing-fee math

What if each runway use paid something?

The City charges nothing for the runway today. The ledger asks a simple counterfactual, with arithmetic plain enough for anyone to check.

Step 1 — the observed daily rate daily rate = runway uses in the window ÷ 30 days
Step 2 — apply a fee you choose annual revenue = daily rate × fee × billable share × 365

The fee is a slider on the live site — presets of $5 (Conservative), $10 (Fair-use, the default), and $15 (Impact), running from $0 to $30. The billable share is a second slider (default 100%) that models exemptions such as based-aircraft discounts or training waivers — a modeling assumption you control, not a figure the data reports.

Illustrative — 3,000 is a stand-in so the arithmetic is easy to follow. The live ledger runs this same formula on the real observed count. Where zero uses are observed, it honestly shows $0: the arithmetic is not broken, there was simply nothing to count.

The one number that is not a floor

The projected annual figure

Projected, not counted

The headline “projected annual” figure extrapolates our own measured rate across a full year:

projected annual runway uses = (uses to date ÷ days counting) × 365

Unlike everything else on the page, this one carries an over-estimate risk, not a floor. Our data so far sits in Colorado’s peak flying season (mid-June into mid-July); winters are quieter, so a full year at this rate is very likely higher than reality — and we say so plainly wherever the number appears. Notably, this count-based projection lands in the same order of magnitude as the FAA’s independent 120,000-operations estimate: two entirely different methods, by two different parties, arriving at a comparable scale.

Locals vs. visitors

How an aircraft is classified

A common argument for a small airport is that it brings visitors who spend money in town. That is a testable claim — so the ledger sorts aircraft into three buckets, under deliberately cautious rules, because these labels sit next to named businesses.

Local

Based here

Positive evidence the aircraft lives at KLMO — chiefly that we watched it stay overnight, or that it is sitting on the field right now.

Unclassified

We don’t know

Not enough positive evidence either way. This is where most aircraft land — and we show that count openly rather than guessing.

Non-local

A visitor

Reserved for a repeatedly observed arrival from a specific named other airport. In practice this is nearly unreachable — by design.

Every classification ships with a receipt — the specific evidence behind it — and the whole model rests on one principle: absence is never evidence. We never penalize an aircraft for something we failed to see. Only things we positively observed can move a classification:

  • Overnight stays An aircraft we watched land and not leave for 8+ hours almost certainly sleeps here. This is the strongest sign of a home aircraft.
  • Still on the field If the last thing we saw was a landing with no departure since, we treat it as still parked here — and we will not call such an aircraft a visitor.
  • Where arrivals come from The only signal that can argue an aircraft is a visitor — and it requires repeatedly watching it fly in from a specific named airport.
  • Registered city If the FAA registry lists the aircraft’s mailing city as Longmont, that nudges toward local. A registrant in another town is not counted against it.

The model is intentionally asymmetric. A false “local” label slightly understates the ledger and costs no one anything. A false “non-local” label is a public accusation against a named business — so we take the harmless error every time. We would rather leave a genuine visitor unlabeled for another month than wrongly brand a local business a visitor for a day.

Who actually stopped

A runway use is not a visit

Arriving at a runway is not the same as stopping in Longmont — and the ledger measures the difference.

Most

of all runway use is an aircraft that never stopped at all — touch-and-goes and low approaches that touched down (or didn’t) and were airborne again in seconds. The live figure is shown as an exact percentage of the window’s total.

For the landings that did stop, we measure time on the ground by pairing each landing with that aircraft’s next takeoff. A stay of 20 minutes or more counts as an aircraft that “actually stopped and did something” — refueled, ate, dropped someone off. That 20-minute line is our own judgment call, not an FAA standard, and we label it as such.

We only report this for landings where we could see both the arrival and the departure, and we publish that coverage fraction openly. The real number of true visits is higher than what we can prove — never lower.

Naming operators — and protecting people

Businesses are named. People are not.

The ledger ranks activity by operator (the business), never by tail number. An N-number resolves to an owner’s name and home address in a single FAA lookup, and a public per-aircraft money leaderboard would be a harassment vector aimed at individual pilots — some of them students in rented aircraft.

So only organizations are named — flight schools, skydiving operators, clubs, corporations, government. Individually owned aircraft, including single-member LLCs and trusts (just how a person holds a personal plane), are pooled into a “Private / unaffiliated” bucket and never named. No registrant street address is ever published.

Why this matters for the City

The excuse this ledger dissolves

When Longmont accepts federal airport money, it signs a promise about what the airport’s fee structure has to try to be.

$725,000

The FAA awarded Vance Brand Municipal Airport this sum in 2026, under the Airport Improvement Program, to reconstruct a taxilane.

Longmont accepted $725,000 in federal money this year to rebuild a taxilane, while charging nothing at all for the runway.

The historic reason untowered fields charge nothing is precisely economy of collection — with no tower and no staff, counting who used the runway once cost more than it could raise. This ledger dissolves that excuse. It counts runway use with no tower, no staff, and no budget beyond public ADS-B data. Collection is now an arithmetic problem, not a staffing problem — and any revenue raised would, by the same federal rules, be locked to the airport itself.
